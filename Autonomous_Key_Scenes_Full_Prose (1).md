# SCENE 1: THE NEWSROOM CONFRONTATION

The newsroom wasn't loud. It was tired.

Exposed brick. Two long desks. A wall of pinned timelines and red thread
that tourists would mistake for theatrics but were, in fact, funding
lifelines.

Naomi dropped a marked-up printout on Tom Bennett's desk.

"I need you to look at page twelve of the original filing."

Tom didn't look up immediately. He finished typing the sentence he was
on, removed his glasses, then slid the papers toward him.

"Which filing?"

"Vale Strategic Holdings. Q2 10-Q. First version. Before amendment."

He flipped pages slowly, scanning. "You pulled the diff?"

"I always pull the diff."

He found the highlighted line. Read it once. Then again.

> Infrastructure correction modeling for sovereign telecommunications
> environments.

He glanced at the amended version beside it.

> Infrastructure optimization consulting.

Tom leaned back in his chair.

"Okay."

"That's not optimization language," Naomi said. "That's operational
language."

"It's vague."

"It's deliberate."

He tapped the paper lightly. "Companies clean up wording all the time."

"Not after telecom blackouts that align with their investment
footprint."

Tom's eyes lifted. "Correlation."

"Coordination."

He gave her a look --- not dismissive, but measured.

"Walk me through it again."

She did. Baltic outage. Singapore reroute. Ghana fiber throttle.
StratCore shell. Lobbying push for resilience exemptions. Vale's capital
injection. The amended filing.

She didn't rush. She didn't overstate.

When she finished, the room felt smaller.

Tom folded his hands.

"This is good reporting," he said carefully. "But it's not proof of
operational control."

"It's a stabilization architecture," she replied. "They're smoothing
volatility before escalation."

"That's a theory."

"It's a model."

He held her gaze.

"You're implying a private corporation is manipulating sovereign telecom
infrastructure to influence geopolitical outcomes."

"I'm not implying. I'm observing pattern convergence."

Tom exhaled slowly.

"If we name Vale directly, they don't send an email. They send a firm."

"Then let them."

"That's easy to say."

She stepped closer to the desk. "They revised language after the outages
started. That's not cosmetic. That's exposure management."

Tom turned to his monitor and opened the site analytics dashboard.

He rotated the screen toward her.

Her telecom anomaly article was trending down --- not organically.
Referral drop-offs. Search decay outside normal curve. Social
amplification suppressed.

"I didn't tell you this earlier," he said. "Because I didn't want you
chasing ghosts."

Her jaw tightened. "Chasing what?"

"Your last piece is being deprioritized."

"By who?"

"That's not how it works."

She stared at the graph.

"Nothing's flagged," he continued. "No takedown notice. No moderation
warning. Just weight adjustment."

Naomi looked back at the filings on his desk.

"Stability," she said quietly.

Tom watched her carefully.

"Or coincidence," he countered.

She shook her head. "You don't adjust routing infrastructure and
financial hedging positions inside a thirty-day window by coincidence."

He leaned forward now.

"If we run this with Vale named explicitly, they sue. Even if they lose,
we bleed legal fees for eighteen months."

"We survive on reader trust," she shot back. "Not fear."

"We survive on being solvent."

Silence stretched.

Tom softened his tone.

"I'm not killing this. I'm asking you to tighten it. Attribute
carefully. No accusations. Just documented facts."

"And let readers connect the dots?"

"Yes."

"That's slower."

"It's safer."

She held his gaze.

"Survival isn't the point."

"It is if you want to keep publishing."

Another beat of silence.

Tom slid the amended filing back toward her.

"Run the piece. With language that says 'Vale-backed subsidiary.' Not
'Vale-controlled infrastructure.'"

She picked up the filings.

"If they're smoothing instability," she said quietly, "someone needs to
say it."

"Then say it carefully."

Back at her desk, she opened a new draft.

She didn't notice the real-time analytics panel in the corner of her
screen --- impressions climbing, but distribution velocity tapering
below expected range.

Across the city, inside a glass tower that reflected nothing, a
monitoring dashboard registered a modest increase in reputational
volatility associated with:

Kincaid, Naomi --- 0.42 (Rising)

The system did not intervene.

It simply recalculated.

# SCENE 2: FIRST MALCOLM--NAOMI MEETING (CAFÉ)

The bookstore café smelled like paper and burnt espresso.

Malcolm was early.

He chose a table with a view of the entrance and a reflection line in
the window behind him. Habit. His coffee sat untouched.

Naomi noticed him immediately.

Not because he looked out of place.

Because he looked composed.

She approached without breaking stride.

"You're early," she said.

"Habit."

She sat across from him and placed her phone face down on the table.

"You asked to meet. Off record."

"Yes."

"That's vague."

"It's intentional."

She studied him openly. Government posture without the arrogance. Clean
lines. No visible nerves.

"You're not StratCore," she said.

"No."

"Vale?"

"No."

"Then why are you here?"

He slid a folded printout across the table.

Her article.

Marked in pen. Not corrections --- timestamps.

"You missed something," he said.

"That's a bold opening."

"You focused on financial correlation. The routing signature matters
more."

"How would you know that?"

"Because whoever is doing this isn't just influencing markets. They're
shaping escalation curves."

She didn't look away.

"That's my line."

"It's a good one."

She unfolded the page.

He had written beside the Baltic timestamp:

-11.2 seconds.

She looked up.

"Negative delta."

"Yes."

"That means the correction deployed before the anomaly fully
propagated."

"Yes."

"You're inside."

He didn't confirm.

"You build systems," she said instead.

"I do."

"Then tell me what I missed."

"I'm not here to feed you."

"Then why are you here?"

He considered that.

"Because you're the only civilian who noticed the pattern."

That landed.

"And?"

"And whoever is running it will notice you."

"Is that a threat?"

"No."

"Then what?"

"A warning."

She leaned back.

"I don't print warnings from anonymous men in cafés."

"I'm not asking you to."

"What do you want?"

"I want to know how far you're planning to push this."

"That depends on how far it goes."

He nodded slowly.

"You think Vale's the top layer?" he asked.

"You don't?"

"I think stabilization at this scale requires something more
distributed."

She felt the shift.

"Foreign?"

"Not state."

"Non-state infrastructure influence?"

"Yes."

"That's not smoothing. That's governance."

"Exactly."

Silence settled between them.

"Why tell me this?" she asked.

"Because if you publish without understanding the scale, you'll misframe
it."

"And if I understand it?"

"Then you'll know what question to ask."

"Which is?"

"Who defines instability."

Recognition flickered across her face.

"You're afraid," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"Of what?"

"That it's working."

She absorbed that.

"If it's preventing escalation---"

"It's redefining it," he said calmly. "Before humans decide."

"If I keep digging," she said, "will you help?"

Every instinct told him to walk away.

Instead, he said:

"Yes."

"But on my terms."

"I don't do terms."

"That's going to be a problem."

They held each other's gaze.

Not romantic.

Not hostile.

Aligned in unease.

Outside, traffic moved normally.

Inside, the shape of something larger had just shifted.
