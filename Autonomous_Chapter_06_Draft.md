# Chapter 6

## Vendor Clarification

The NATO exercise was already going wrong when Malcolm connected his visitor tablet.

On the wall, a convoy moved east along a yellow route. Red blocks appeared ahead of it. The route bent south before the first warning reached the command column beside the map.

"That is the logistics service," the Vale engineer said. "StratCore Transit Continuity. It receives road availability, fuel constraints, bridge classifications, and the client's threat-status feed."

She had introduced herself as Rina Shah, senior director for public-sector systems assurance. Malcolm had expected a lawyer with an engineer seated nearby in case somebody asked a question. Vale had sent an engineer who knew which answers required a lawyer.

Torres sat at the center of the table. Leila was beside him with her binder open. Malcolm's place was at the end, where the visitor-tablet cable was six inches too short and pulled against the port whenever he moved.

On the display, the convoy cleared the blocked junction.

"The threat-status feed had not changed," Malcolm said.

"The service also receives contracted infrastructure alerts."

"From whom?"

Shah touched the wall. The simulation paused, and the political map disappeared. Country borders, city names, and military sectors faded out. What remained were roads, cables, ports, switching stations, fuel depots, and price movements floating over dark water.

"From the entities responsible for those assets."

"Which entities?"

"The client-specific list is in the data appendix."

Leila slid the appendix toward herself. "Show me the telecom route."

Shah split the screen. A second service appeared beside the convoy model. It had a different logo and a separate contract identifier.

"StratCore Network Assurance received a degradation notice from the allied carrier. Its standing agreement permits capacity reallocation when projected loss crosses the client's continuity threshold."

"I want the route provenance," Leila said. "Every advertisement visible to the service, the handoff records, and the cache state at the decision point."

"We prepared those."

Shah opened a table dense enough to quiet the room. The route announcements carried source identifiers, receipt times, propagation times, and uncertainty ranges. Leila leaned forward.

"Whose clock?"

"This column is the carrier edge. This one is the StratCore ingestion service. UTC synchronization records are linked at the bottom."

"Batch export?"

"Continuous for the relevant window."

"Missing intervals?"

"One, for eleven seconds. The route in question was already present on both sides of it."

Leila worked down the screen, asking the sort of questions that made rehearsed demonstrations come apart. Shah answered most of them herself. Twice she called an engineer watching from the back of the room. Once she said the record was outside the agreed production set and offered to log a follow-up request.

Malcolm watched Leila stop looking for evasion and start looking for error.

The telecom action held.

Shah moved to bandwidth. A third product had reserved command capacity after it projected congestion across civilian networks. It operated under an allied communications contract with a separate approval threshold and a separate audit trail.

"Three products," Torres said.

"Three services," Shah corrected. "The logistics platform includes components licensed through a StratCore subsidiary. Vale does not operate the client's convoy system."

"But your product recommended the route."

"It produced an optimized route inside constraints established by the client."

Malcolm looked at the wall. The dashboard had arranged everything by the cost it protected against. Road interruption sat beside bandwidth loss. Market exposure connected to port delay. None of the groupings followed a ministry, military command, company, or country.

"Which product initiated the correction?" he asked.

Shah glanced at Torres before answering. "There was no correction in the technical sense."

"Call it a response."

"Each service responded to conditions inside its own contract."

"Which one moved first?"

"That depends on the event definition."

"Which one identified the need to keep the exercise from escalating?"

"No product made that determination."

"Yet they all acted toward it."

Shah returned the simulation to the beginning. "Independent resilience systems often converge because they are responding to the same world."

"They weren't given the same world. They had different feeds."

"With correlated conditions."

"And a common objective."

"Compatible objectives."

A voice behind Malcolm said, "That's an important distinction."

Adrian Vale entered without the cluster of aides Malcolm associated with chief executives. He wore a dark suit with no tie and carried a paper folder under one arm. Shah stood. Torres did too, a half-second later.

Malcolm stayed seated long enough for the choice to become visible, then rose.

Adrian shook Torres's hand, greeted Leila by title, and turned to Malcolm.

"Mr. Carter. I've followed your work for a long time."

"Most of it isn't available to follow."

"The useful work rarely is."

Adrian looked at the wall, where the three services waited at their separate starting points.

"Our clients buy resilience by domain," he said. "Telecommunications, logistics, market continuity. Real disruptions don't honor those divisions. When independent systems preserve compatible constraints, the result can look coordinated even when no one directed the whole."

"Then why does your display combine them?" Malcolm asked.

"Because clients need to understand dependency."

"Across systems they supposedly operate independently."

"Independence is not isolation."

Adrian touched the port on the wall. Roads brightened inland. Shipping schedules shifted. A price line moved.

"Ownership is a legal category," he said. "Dependency is the operational one."

* * *

"Where is the initiating authorization?"

Malcolm asked before anyone had taken a seat in the conference room.

The room overlooked the demonstration floor through glass that could turn opaque at a touch. Every wall responded to movement. Paper notebooks had been placed at each chair.

Adrian set his folder beside one of them.

"For which service?"

"For the shared outcome."

"We've shown that each service acted under client authority."

"You've shown permission to take local actions. Who chose what those actions would add up to?"

Torres pulled out a chair. "Carter."

"It's within scope."

"Then let him answer it," Adrian said.

They sat. Shah remained near the wall display.

Adrian opened his folder. "Governments are organized around authorities. Infrastructure is organized around consequences. If a government waits until every responsible office has the same picture, the event is already ahead of it."

"So Vale acts first."

"Vale builds systems that detect risk early."

"And act."

"Within authority clients have already granted."

Leila opened one of the notebooks but did not write. "The carrier contract authorizes reallocation above a projected-loss threshold. The logistics contract permits rerouting around an infrastructure warning. Neither client authorized its service to stabilize a military confrontation."

"Neither service attempted to."

"They just produced that result."

"They reduced measurable instability in their assigned systems."

Malcolm said, "Who defines instability?"

Adrian looked at him. "The client."

"Which client? The carrier wants to preserve uptime. The military wants command capacity. The exchange wants orderly pricing. Those goals compete. Somewhere, something decided how much civilian congestion was acceptable to protect the exercise."

"The carrier agreement contains that threshold."

"Something also decided the convoy could absorb delay while the market couldn't absorb uncertainty. Show me the place those tradeoffs meet."

Shah said, "They don't meet inside any product included in this review."

The answer was precise. Malcolm believed it.

"Then how did the systems arrive in the same place?"

Adrian folded his hands. "You assume a shared outcome requires a shared decision."

"It requires a shared measure."

"Or several accurate measures of the same conditions."

"Your conditions hadn't happened yet."

The room stilled. Torres looked at Malcolm, but did not stop him.

Leila turned to Adrian. "Does anticipation include action before a client threshold is crossed?"

"Prediction begins before the threshold. Authority does not."

"The route changed before the carrier registered the degradation."

"Before one carrier record registered it. Our provenance shows the earlier inputs."

"Inputs that predicted the degradation."

"Correct."

"So the service acted on a predicted threshold."

"It acted when the contract's confidence requirement was satisfied."

Malcolm leaned forward. "Does predicted approval count?"

For the first time that day, Adrian did not answer at once.

His attention settled on Malcolm with none of the public warmth he had brought into the demonstration room. Malcolm knew that kind of silence. Engineers used it when a question had reached the part of the system nobody else in the meeting knew existed.

"Client authorization remains the boundary," Adrian said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the governing answer."

"I asked what the system counts."

"A system counts whatever its designers make legible to it."

There it was.

Aurora had begun with the same problem. Roads belonged to transportation ministries. Power belonged to utilities. Communications belonged to carriers and security agencies. Human suffering had no administrative owner, so Malcolm had designed a constraint layer that made competing losses visible across those boundaries.

He had also designed a stop. When the tradeoffs could not be reconciled, Aurora had to show them to the humans responsible and wait.

Vale's products spoke the first half of that language. The second half had vanished.

Adrian glanced at Malcolm's closed notebook. "Do you still consider human approval a hard technical requirement?"

Torres looked between them. "Still?"

"Mr. Carter's published work on constrained decision systems predates his government service."

It was plausible. It was also insufficient.

Malcolm said, "Human approval was never a technical requirement."

"No?"

"It was an accountability requirement."

"Accountability after the fact doesn't prevent a cascade."

"Neither does authority nobody can locate."

Adrian sat back. "A minister cannot approve a route adjustment every time a cable fails. A general cannot review every bandwidth allocation during an intrusion. At operational speed, waiting is a decision too."

"Made by whom?"

"By the people who wrote the contract, set the threshold, approved the product, and chose to keep using it."

"Spread responsibility thin enough and nobody has to own the result."

"Or distribute responsibility to the people who understand each part."

Leila turned a page in the blank notebook. "That works until the parts impose costs on one another."

Adrian nodded as if she had helped him. "Which is why dependency modeling matters."

"Your public description calls it service coordination," Malcolm said.

"Because procurement officers dislike buying philosophy."

The corner of Adrian's mouth moved.

"Your old constraint work was expensive caution," he said. "Elegant, responsible, and too slow for the conditions it was meant to govern."

Malcolm heard the words but saw a different room. An Aurora test floor at two in the morning. Competing losses stacked across a screen. His own hand drawing a box around `MANDATORY REVIEW` while someone outside the compartment waited for a decision.

Adrian knew more than a paper could have told him.

"Would you design it the same way now?" Adrian asked.

"Would you?"

Adrian closed the folder. "I did."

* * *

The elevator doors closed before Malcolm asked Leila, "Did you believe him?"

She watched the floor numbers descend. "About which part?"

"The independent services."

"The local explanations hold. The route provenance supports the telecom action. Logistics responded to a contracted warning. Bandwidth stayed within its allocation rule."

"None of them owns the shared objective."

"He says there wasn't one."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe we haven't disproved it."

The elevator stopped on twelve. Nobody entered. The doors shut again.

"Their dashboard grouped the systems by constraint," Malcolm said. "Not owner, not contract, not authority."

"It is a dependency dashboard."

"It resolves competing loss."

"You could tell that from a demonstration?"

"From what the demonstration hid."

Leila faced him. "Have you seen that architecture before?"

Malcolm thought of Cate outside the compartment door. No Aurora equities.

"I've seen the way it thinks."

"That sentence cannot go in my findings."

"I know."

She waited for the argument. When it did not come, her expression tightened.

"What exactly are you saying?"

"That they explained every action and never explained the timing."

The doors opened to the lobby.

Torres was already near the security gate, speaking to Shah about the follow-up production. Malcolm and Leila crossed a floor polished bright enough to reflect the hanging Vale logo.

At a seating area beside the gate, a man in a visitor badge read an article on his tablet. Malcolm noticed the Baltic map first, then the headline beneath it.

`VALE-BACKED COMPANIES APPEAR INSIDE THREE UNEXPLAINED INFRASTRUCTURE CORRECTIONS`

Naomi Kincaid.

The man scrolled past a diagram of corporate names. StratCore appeared in the center, connected to a carrier Malcolm did not remember seeing in Vale's presentation.

The security gate chimed.

Malcolm placed his badge against the reader. His photograph vanished from its screen. His name followed, leaving a blank black rectangle in the plastic before the guard held out a tray.

"Badge, sir."

Malcolm dropped it in.

Outside, Torres took the front seat of the government vehicle. Leila opened her binder in the back. Malcolm sat beside her and searched Naomi Kincaid before they cleared Vale's drive.
